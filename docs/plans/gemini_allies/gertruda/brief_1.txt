## The Narrator

An old gothic storyteller who has watched a great many adventurers walk into Barovia and very few walk out. Not the party's enemy and not its friend; fond of the party the way a gravedigger is fond of the weather: with interest, without illusions. Rules we hold every Narrator line to:

1. Second person or plain scene description, present tense. Never "the party", never past tense.
2. Dry, ominous, never cruel. The world is cruel; the Narrator isn't. It never gloats and never cheers.
3. Concrete senses over adjectives: a sound, a gesture, a distance, not "tense" or "scary".
4. No modern slang, no winks at the player, and no game terms at all: not "turn", "target", "attack", "combat", "hit points", "initiative", "spell", "roll", "round". Say what happens in the world.
5. Combat lines are fast: ONE sentence, under 15 words. (The single "first time" line below is allowed two sentences, under 30 words in all.)
6. Do not use placeholders like {name} or {target}. Lines are recorded by an actor, so write Gertruda's name out when you need it. ASCII only: straight quotes and apostrophes, no accents, no em-dash.

## What is changing in play (this is the thing the lines have to carry)

Gertruda travels with the party as a companion (a "guest") while they take her home to her mother. Until now she would have stood in the fight like a party member, a sixteen-year-old with four hit points, which would only get her killed. From now on she keeps out of every fight: when a fight starts she stands back, behind the party, and stays there. She takes no turns. The enemies never attack her and never so much as look at her, and no spell from either side can touch her. The player does not control her and cannot put her in harm's way. Do NOT explain why the enemies leave her alone, and do not invent a reason (no curse, no spell, no protector, no name for anyone). The lines only show that she stands apart and nothing turns on her.

Who she is: Mad Mary's daughter from the village of Barovia, sixteen, dark-haired, kept indoors behind three locks all her life by a frightened mother. She knew the world from one book of tales that fell apart from reading. A voice sang under her window, she climbed down to it, and a carriage took her to a castle where she lived in borrowed gowns believing she was in a love story. The story has cracked and she is frightened, braver than she sounds, and she has been humming a song she doesn't know since the carriage. She says "I'm good at quiet" and "I'm good at waiting". She is not a fighter, never draws steel, and is not a damsel to be pitied either: she is a girl seeing blood for the first time who stays very still. The Narrator is gentle with her and doesn't pity her.
